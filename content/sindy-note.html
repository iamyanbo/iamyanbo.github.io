Suppose a few sensors go missing and the remaining readings are noisy. We would like to recover the equations behind the motion, but first we have to decide what information the measurements actually contain.

The construction

I combined an echo state network with SINDy, using simulated trajectories with controlled masking and noise. The reservoir model fits the observed trajectory; repeated noisy rollouts supply an uncertainty estimate for generated points. The pipeline filters those points and compares equation discovery on the original observations with equation discovery on the augmented data.

SINDy fits state derivatives to a library of candidate functions and uses sparse regression to select a small set of terms. For Lorenz, the useful terms include x, y, z, xy, and xz. Recovering those terms gives an equation we can inspect, as well as a predictor we can simulate.

What the comparison found

The repository reports marginal improvements across its nine configured systems. The displayed Lorenz fits are almost identical. For the x-equation, the original-data fit is 0.065 − 9.956x + 9.955y; the augmented-data fit is 0.075 − 9.956x + 9.956y. The reference is −10x + 10y.

Those coefficients illustrate the reported comparison; they are not a new evaluation carried out for this portfolio. The public repository contains the implementation and reported fits, rather than a complete run-by-run results archive.

What I took from it

A predicted trajectory is not an independent observation. When the reservoir has too little reliable data, its predictions can carry the same uncertainty and bias into the equation fit. When the original data is already sufficient, the generated points may add little useful information. The experiment made that distinction much more concrete for me.
