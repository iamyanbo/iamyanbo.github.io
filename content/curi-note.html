A loop that changes code until a benchmark improves can be useful engineering. I wanted to explore a different loop: ask what is happening, consider competing explanations, and design a check that can tell them apart.

How the system works

A watcher reads and admits relevant sources. An orchestrator chooses a question and proposes an experiment. A gate rejects proposals that have no connection to prior evidence or repeat an earlier study. An executor implements the admitted experiment in its own Git worktree, and the runtime independently reruns the decisive checks.

The result returns to the orchestrator for interpretation. Findings accumulate in a persistent record, and later evidence can revise a synthesis. If there is nothing worth investigating, the system can pause. A higher score is not required for an experiment to leave a useful result.

A concrete example

The attention direction investigated KV-cache eviction, quantization, and speculative decoding. One study found that a draft model could make decoding slower even with a reasonable token acceptance rate. Another refuted the run’s own hypothesis about cumulative attention as a way to retain information needed by later questions.

These were bounded reproductions and engineering studies. The attention experiments used 0.5B–1.5B models at context lengths up to 4,096 on one RTX 3060 Ti. They do not establish new general laws about frontier models.

The part I wanted to make inspectable

The question, the proposed falsifier, the code, the checks, and the interpretation should stay connected. CURI keeps that record, accounts for model-call costs, and publishes a redacted, read-only mirror. The contribution I am exploring is the research process; whether it can produce findings beyond the existing literature remains an open question.
